An AI-Era Testing Strategy

Quality isn't a final gate. It's built in from the first line.

AI increases the pace and scale of change. Quality depends on understanding risk, gathering trustworthy evidence and learning from real outcomes. The whole team builds quality; AI accelerates the work; QA connects the context and assures the outcome.

Start here · why bother testing early

A bug gets pricier every step it survives.

Caught in a unit test, a defect costs a coffee break. The same defect caught in production costs a war room. That single curve is the reason for everything that follows.

So the strategy is to catch each defect at the earliest, smallest, cheapest check that can provide enough confidence. The right check depends on the risk and the system.

Example · a risk-based way to scale testing

Risk decides how deep you test.

Not every change needs the same evidence. This low / medium / high grouping is one example: scale testing to impact, reversibility, and what is at stake. Adapt the categories to the product and its risks.

Low risk

Prove it and move

Isolated, reversible, small impact. Unit and component coverage plus a focused check is enough. Don't gold-plate it.

Medium risk

Widen the net

Crosses a service, adds a data flow, or is a significant journey. Add contracts, integration and regression coverage.

High risk

Deepen evidence; QA decides

For example, changes involving security, money, identity, irreversible data or compliance may need deeper negative, performance and resilience checks, a staged rollout, and QA sign-off.

Principle · test at the lowest level that gives confidence

Where should we check?

Catch defects as close as practical to their source. Choose the lowest level that gives enough confidence for the risk; the mix of levels depends on the system.

  • Unit — pure logic, one class or function.
  • Component — one deployable service via its API, boundaries mocked.
  • Integration — only when two or more parts together beat isolation.
  • System — the whole thing, end to end.
  • Acceptance — business and regulatory sign-off.

This pyramid is one example, not a required shape or quota. Adapt the levels to architecture and risk; integration-heavy systems may lean more on contracts and component tests.

Principle · combine dimensions of evidence

What should we protect—and how?

Decide where to check, what could go wrong, and how to find out. These choices work together; they are not competing kinds of tests.

1 · WHERE?At what level should we check it? A service, or the whole user journey?
2 · WHAT?What matters most to protect? For example, security, speed, or ease of use.
3 · HOW?How will we find problems? Automated checks, hands-on exploration, or review?
Example · changing sign-in Where: check the sign-in service and the full login journey. What: check account security and whether people can still sign in. How: automate permission checks, then explore unusual user paths.

These examples overlap: security is a concern, exploratory is an approach, and end-to-end describes scope. Give each relevant risk a place in the strategy.

Principles · make evidence trustworthy

Trust the evidence.

These principles help keep test evidence useful. The specific checks and thresholds are examples to adapt to risk, architecture and context.

01

Required checks pass

Agree which checks block a merge. For example, a team may require critical unit and component checks to pass and use coverage thresholds to flag gaps—not as proof of quality on their own.

02

Deterministic & independent

No shared mutable state. No ordering or timing dependence. No sleeping and hoping. A test must prove something every run.

03

Challenge assumptions

Consider invalid inputs, boundaries and failure conditions. Boundary-value and equivalence analysis are useful techniques, chosen according to the risk.

04

Every check earns its place

Keep evidence that protects a distinct risk or critical rule. For example, quarantine flaky checks with a reason, owner and expiry; remove duplicates and obsolete cases.

The rule that's easiest to break

Make checks independent.

Keeping tests deterministic and independent sounds simple, until two suites share an environment that never resets. That's where green tests turn flaky. So tests are written to assume the world is already dirty.

Shared counter starts at 7. Run a test:

✕ assert count == 10
✓ assert count increased by 10

Same run, two assertions. The absolute check breaks the moment someone else touched the data; the delta check survives.

  1. Run‑unique IDsnever reuse a fixed record two suites both write to.
  2. Delta, not absolutecapture state at start, assert on the change you made.
  3. Clean what you canrecords, artifacts, config overrides, all torn down.
  4. Never touch productiontest data stays out of prod, full stop.

The other half of a trustworthy test

Build for observability.

A feature isn't fully testable until it exposes enough evidence to prove it worked, in a test and later in production. Observability is a requirement of the feature, not an afterthought.

See it

Meaningful signals

Events, error codes and health endpoints that say what happened, not just that something did.

Trace it

Correlation across boundaries

A shared ID that follows a request through every service, so a failure can be traced end to end.

Measure it

Success defined up front

The technical and business signals that say the change worked: error rate, latency, the key outcome.

Set it up

Safe test hooks

API setup paths and visible feature-flag state, so tests arrange data fast and read the system's real state.

Good observability pays off three times: reliable automated checks, faster diagnosis, and safer canaries.

Past the gate · release is the start, not the end

Release in stages. Learn from real outcomes.

Test environments never match production exactly, so some defects only show under real traffic. Don't ship to everyone at once. Expose the change to a small slice, watch the signals, then promote it, or roll it back before most users ever notice.

Example · scale AI controls with autonomy and risk

Bound AI autonomy by risk.

This ladder is one way to think about AI permissions: as AI takes on more consequential actions, require stronger controls. Adapt the rungs and safeguards to the task and its risk.

Assist Analyse code, explain impact, draft requirements and test cases Check assumptions, context and impact
Generate Turn requirements into tests and implementation changes Independent tests, static & security checks, code review
Repair Diagnose failures and propose repairs Runs only in isolated envs; a person approves the change
Release support Summarise evidence, recommend promote or roll back QA reviews the evidence and owns the release verdict
Production action Never autonomous by default Risk tier, least privilege, dry-run, approval, audit trail, circuit breaker

Every rung needs data guardrails: protect sensitive information, use approved models, treat repository and ticket content as untrusted input, and keep provenance and decisions auditable.

Ownership at machine scale

Everyone builds quality. QA owns the release verdict.

QA does not review every generated line. It connects domain context to independent evidence, makes gaps visible, and decides whether the product is ready to release.

The whole team builds it

  • Engineering designs and automates for quality
  • Product defines outcomes and residual risk
  • Security and operations own specialised controls and signals
→

QA owns assurance

  • Connects customer, business and technical context to critical expectations
  • Assesses evidence against risk; unknowns are not passes
  • Owns the quality verdict and blocks release when the evidence is not sufficient

Automation may pause or roll back against pre-agreed safety thresholds; QA owns the decision to approve or block release.

Why independence matters: if only admins may delete a record, write that expectation first. Otherwise, AI can allow any signed-in user and generate a test that confirms the same mistake—green, but wrong.
